Understanding the weight of thinking

Mental ergonomics studies how cognitive work is structured. Allostatic cognitive load describes how that work accumulates and recovers. The Cognitive Burden Index is a transparent, adjustable model that combines both into a single interpretable signal.

What this is about

Mental Ergonomics

The design of cognitive work so that attention, memory, and decision-making are used efficiently and sustainably. Poor mental ergonomics creates unnecessary switching, overload, and error.

Allostatic Load

The cumulative “wear and tear” that results from repeated adaptation to demand. When recovery does not keep pace with load, allostatic load rises — first cognitively, then physiologically.

Cognitive Burden Index

A composite score (0–100) that estimates present cognitive burden from measurable or estimable factors. It is designed to be transparent, adjustable, and educational — not a clinical diagnosis.

The Mathematics

The Cognitive Burden Index is deliberately simple and fully transparent. Every term is visible and adjustable. This is an exploratory model for understanding, not a validated clinical instrument.

1. Static Snapshot Index

Each input factor is already normalized to the interval $[0,1]$. Recovery Opportunity is inverted to become a deficit:

$$ RD = 1 - RO $$

The static Cognitive Burden Index is a weighted linear combination:

$$ CBI_{\text{static}} = 100 \times \big( w_{TS}\,TS + w_{WM}\,WM + w_{TP}\,TP + w_{ID}\,ID + w_{EL}\,EL + w_{RD}\,RD \big) $$

Default weights (sum to 1):

Task SwitchingwTS = 0.20
Working MemorywWM = 0.22
Time PressurewTP = 0.18
Interruption DensitywID = 0.15
Emotional LoadwEL = 0.12
Recovery DeficitwRD = 0.13

These weights are starting points based on cognitive load theory and common occupational patterns. They can (and should) be revised with domain data.

2. Dynamic Allostatic Component

Allostatic load grows when demand exceeds recovery and declines when recovery exceeds demand. A minimal continuous-time description is:

$$ \frac{dL}{dt} = \alpha \cdot Load(t) - \beta \cdot Recovery(t) $$

where $L$ is latent allostatic cognitive load, $\alpha$ is the accumulation rate, and $\beta$ is the recovery rate.

In the interactive model we use a discrete approximation over short steps:

$$ L_{t+1} = L_t + \Delta t \big( \alpha \cdot CBI_{\text{static}}/100 - \beta \cdot RO \big) $$

and then map the latent load back to a 0–100 display scale with a soft saturation:

$$ CBI_{\text{dynamic}} = 100 \times \frac{L}{L + k} $$

where $k$ is a scaling constant that keeps the index from exploding. This produces the characteristic allostatic behavior: sustained high demand with poor recovery drives the index upward even if instantaneous demand is only moderate.

3. Zone Interpretation

  • 0–25 — Low — Cognitive resources are largely available. Sustainable for extended periods.
  • 26–50 — Moderate — Normal operational load. Monitor recovery across days.
  • 51–75 — Elevated — Significant demand. Error rates and fatigue risk rise if prolonged.
  • 76–100 — High — Heavy burden. Strongly indicates need for load reduction or recovery.

4. Design Principles Behind the Model

  • Transparency — Every term is visible and editable.
  • Separability — Demand factors and recovery are kept distinct so interventions can be targeted.
  • Bounded output — The index is forced into [0, 100] for interpretability.
  • Educational first — The model prioritizes understanding over predictive optimality.

Conceptual Frameworks

Cognitive Load Theory (Sweller)

Distinguishes intrinsic load (inherent difficulty of the material), extraneous load (imposed by poor design), and germane load (effort devoted to schema formation). Mental ergonomics primarily attacks extraneous load while protecting germane processing.

Allostatic Load (McEwen)

Originally physiological, the concept extends naturally to cognition: repeated activation of stress and executive systems without adequate recovery produces cumulative cost. The dynamic term in the CBI is a direct homage to this idea.

Mental Ergonomics

Applies ergonomic thinking to cognitive work: reduce unnecessary switching, match demand to capacity, design for recovery, and make system state visible to the person doing the work.

Why a composite index?

Single metrics (e.g. “hours worked” or “number of emails”) miss interactions. A transparent composite forces us to name the factors we believe matter and to confront their relative weights.

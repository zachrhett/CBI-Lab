Conceptual frameworks

Deeper background for the two pillars this lab synthesizes: cognitive ergonomics (how mental work is structured) and allostatic load (how the organism pays for repeated adaptation). This page is educational context for a personal model — not a claim of clinical validation.

Primary literature is listed under References & studies. Terms are defined in the Glossary.

1. Cognitive ergonomics

Cognitive ergonomics (also called cognitive engineering in related traditions) is the branch of ergonomics / human factors concerned with mental processes — perception, memory, reasoning, and motor response — as they interact with work systems, interfaces, and environments. Where classical physical ergonomics asks whether a chair or lift is safe for the body, cognitive ergonomics asks whether the information structure of a task is compatible with human attention, working memory, and decision limits.

International standards frames (e.g. ISO 6385 on ergonomics principles, ISO 10075 on mental workload) treat mental workload as multidimensional: demands can be quantitative (how much), qualitative (how complex), and temporal (how paced), and effects show up in performance, subjective strain, and physiological activation. The practical goal is not “zero mental work” but fit — matching task design to human cognitive capacities so demand remains usable rather than error-producing or exhausting.

Intellectual roots (selected)

  • Human factors / engineering psychology — post–World War II work on attention, vigilance, and display design (e.g. traditions associated with researchers such as Donald Broadbent on selective attention, and later Christopher Wickens on multiple-resource models of attention and workload).
  • Cognitive load theory (CLT) — John Sweller and colleagues: instructional and task design should minimize extraneous load (poor structure, unnecessary switching), manage intrinsic load (inherent difficulty), and free capacity for germane processing (learning and schema building).
  • Mental workload measurement — decades of subjective scales (e.g. NASA-TLX; Hart & Staveland), secondary-task methods, and physiological correlates used in aviation, process control, and HCI.

In this lab, “mental ergonomics” is used in that spirit: interruptions, switching, time pressure, working-memory demand, and display-like emotional labor are treated as designable load factors, not moral failures.

2. Allostasis and allostatic load

Homeostasis is the classical idea of defending a fixed set-point (e.g. blood glucose, temperature). Allostasis — a term introduced by Peter Sterling and Joseph Eyer (1988) — emphasizes stability through change: the brain anticipates demand and adjusts physiological systems (autonomic, HPA axis, immune, metabolic) to meet expected load. Adaptation itself is normal and necessary.

Allostatic load, developed especially in the work of Bruce S. McEwen and colleagues (with related population work by researchers such as Teresa Seeman), names the cumulative wear that appears when adaptive systems are turned on too often, fail to shut off, or respond inadequately. The same mediators that protect in the short run (catecholamines, cortisol, inflammatory signals) can damage brain and body when chronically dysregulated — including effects on hippocampus, prefrontal function, sleep, and metabolic health.

Why this matters for “cognitive” burden

Cognitive work is not only a software metaphor. Sustained mental demand, threat appraisal, low controllability, and poor recovery recruit the same stress biology that allostatic-load research studies. Prolonged imbalance is linked — correlationally and mechanistically in animal and human literatures — to fatigue, impaired executive control, mood disturbance, and higher disease risk. McEwen’s framing is useful here because it distinguishes:

  • Allostatic response — adaptive activation and recovery
  • Allostatic load — cumulative cost of repeated or failed adaptation
  • Allostatic overload — when cost becomes frankly pathogenic

That maps cleanly onto this lab’s language of adaptive stress versus erosive / toxic load, and onto a latent accumulation term \(L\) that can climb when recovery does not keep up with demand.

3. Cognitive load theory meets allostasis

Sweller’s cognitive load theory was built mainly for learning environments; McEwen’s allostatic load was built mainly for stress physiology and health. They are not the same theory. This lab borrows structure from both as a personal synthesis:

  • From CLT / cognitive ergonomics: task switching, intrinsic complexity, extraneous interruption, working-memory limits.
  • From allostasis: recovery, sleep, controllability, prolonged activation, cumulative latent load.
  • From occupational health: support, environmental strain, and the idea that workplace systems shape load (e.g. Karasek demand–control ideas as conceptual neighbors).

The Cognitive Burden Index is therefore an exploratory composite: it is transparent and adjustable, but it is not a published psychometric instrument and should not be cited as if it were one.

4. Six arching touch points

The synthesis of cognitive load theory and allostatic load is useful, but it remains somewhat abstract until it is organized into a small set of shared concepts. The six arching touch points below are the places where the three frameworks (CLT, allostatic load, and the Cognitive Burden Index) naturally interlock. They are not additional factors to score; they are the higher-level architecture that the process factors, the latent load \(L\), and the levers all sit inside.

1. Resource allocation & capacity limits

Working memory and attentional bandwidth are finite. Cognitive load theory treats this limit as the central constraint on instructional design. Allostatic models treat it as one expression of a broader regulatory bandwidth that can be depleted across systems. CBI sits at the intersection: it asks how much of available capacity is currently occupied and whether that occupation is sustainable.

2. Cumulative versus acute load

Classical CLT is primarily concerned with the load imposed by a single task or lesson. Allostatic load is concerned with what remains after the task is finished. The Cognitive Burden Index treats both as real. A high static score on one day is informative; a rising latent \(L\) across several days is more informative still. The distinction between a demanding moment and an accumulating trajectory is one of the model’s core design choices.

3. Intrinsic, extraneous, and germane (adaptive) load

Sweller’s tripartite distinction remains useful. Intrinsic load is the complexity inherent in the material or role. Extraneous load is the avoidable cost imposed by poor design, interruption, or noise. Germane load is the constructive processing that builds schema and, in the allostatic framing, can actually support future capacity. CBI inherits this vocabulary and extends it: the same demand can be erosive or adaptive depending on whether recovery and controllability keep pace.

4. Measurement & feedback loops

A model that cannot be observed is not useful for self-regulation. CLT has traditionally used performance, dual-task measures, and subjective ratings. Allostatic research has used biomarker panels. CBI deliberately stays on the educational / self-report side of that spectrum, but it is structured so that the questionnaire, day log, and calculator form a closed feedback loop: observe → interpret → adjust levers → re-observe.

5. Individual differences & baseline calibration

People do not start from the same capacity or the same recovery efficiency. Prior knowledge, working-memory span, sleep quality, personality sensitivities (Big Five / MBPA), and residual medical factors all shift the point at which a given demand becomes costly. CBI treats these differences as calibration parameters rather than as pathology labels. The personality-adjusted weights and optional capacity modifiers exist for this reason.

6. Intervention & load management strategies

The practical endpoint of all three frameworks is the same: keep burden inside a zone that remains adaptive. CLT contributes design principles that reduce extraneous load. Allostatic thinking contributes recovery, sleep, and controllability. CBI contributes the barometer, the trajectory, and the explicit levers so that a person can see which adjustments are likely to matter most right now.

These six concepts are elaborated mathematically on the Mathematics page as a dimensional state space. The process factors, zones, and latent load \(L\) are the observable projections of that space.

6. How CBI uses these ideas

  1. Process factors estimate current exposures (demand, recovery, body, environment).
  2. Low controllability amplifies demand — a Karasek-like interaction inside the toy math.
  3. Barometer zones separate adaptive from erosive ranges.
  4. Latent \(L\) sketches multi-day accumulation when recovery lags.
  5. Levers and libraries point at design and skills — not at identity as defect.
